# Full-text audit batch 11 — closing random-arm topic gaps

Updated 2026-10-11. This batch reviews the last two topic labels not previously represented in the fixed-frame uniform-random arm: model efficiency and causal inference. It also follows the causal paper into subsequent primary work and compares its conclusions with an independent edge-inference calibration study.

## 1. Flexible ensemble inference: latency gains depend on a calibrated decision policy

**Sampled paper:** Tann, Hashemi, and Reda, [Flexible Deep Neural Network Processing](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1801.07353v1), arXiv:1801.07353v1. Sections 3–4. The full paper was read from the author-uploaded copy; arXiv’s PDF endpoint was unavailable in the browser cache during this audit.

The approach executes an ensemble of independently trained networks sequentially, using the current top-two score margin to decide whether another network is worth running. Thresholds are grid-searched on training data against a weighted objective combining runtime and relative error; the paper sets the latency/error weight to α=0.5. On ImageNet with AlexNet, a 7-network flexible ensemble takes 6.51 ms versus 11.67 ms for the normal ensemble, while top-1 accuracy changes from .6043 to .6032 (about 0.11 percentage points). Experiments use AlexNet and ResNet-50, Caffe, and an Intel i7-4790K/Nvidia Titan Xp setup.

**Independent follow-up / countercondition:** Pacheco et al., [Calibration-Aided Edge Inference Offloading via Adaptive Model Partitioning](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2010.16335v1), tests a different early-exit/offload policy on BranchyNet-style AlexNet/CIFAR-10. Its single-exit setup uses 45k training, 3k validation, and 7k test examples. Temperature scaling on validation data improves the reliability of a target-confidence threshold; for target accuracy values below .875, the reported calibrated branch has zero batch-level outage, while the conventional branch reaches outage near .4 at the same targets. Yet calibration is not uniformly best on every system objective: at target accuracy .75, the uncalibrated model may have lower missed-deadline probability because it offloads more often and the accuracy requirement is already met. The study estimates device/cloud timings from separate hardware reports and a Colab GPU, and uses 512-image batches and one dataset/model family.

**Interpretation and limits:** The sampled study demonstrates a favorable latency/accuracy trade on two ImageNet architectures, but its exit thresholds are data-tuned and its policy is not the same as edge offloading. The later study shows why accuracy/latency objectives and calibration must be evaluated jointly: better-calibrated confidence can improve an accuracy guarantee while worsening a deadline objective in a low-accuracy-target regime. Neither paper establishes distribution-shift robustness or current accelerator performance.

**Falsifiable follow-up:** On matched models and hardware, compare fixed-margin, calibrated-margin, and learned/adaptive exit policies under equal calibration budgets. Evaluate in-distribution and shifted inputs across predeclared accuracy and latency targets; report end-to-end accuracy, mean/tail latency, energy, offload rate, outage, and deadline misses. Test whether calibration gains disappear or reverse when the objective changes.

## 2. Causal mediation: extra mechanism data can tighten bounds, but need not

**Sampled record:** Dawid, Murtas, and Musio, [Bounding the Probability of Causation in Mediation Analysis](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1411.2636v1), frame record arXiv:1411.2636v1 (submitted 2014). Sections 2–6.

The paper distinguishes the population question “does exposure change outcome rates?” from an individual “was this observed outcome caused by exposure?” question. Even with randomized exposure and exchangeability, the individual probability of causation is generally only bounded because the two potential outcomes for one person cannot both be observed. In the binary complete-mediation case, under no direct effect and no confounding, mediator data do not improve the lower bound but can improve the upper bound: the worked example tightens a vacuous upper bound to .76 while retaining a .60 lower bound. These are assumption-dependent mathematical bounds, not point estimates from a new human or clinical dataset.

**Follow-up and counterexample:** Murtas, Dawid, and Musio’s [2017 partial-mediation paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/1706.04857) adds direct and indirect pathways under explicit no-confounding assumptions. Its worked examples show the mediator may improve the upper bound (e.g. .60–.76 instead of .60–1), but in another case the mediator-adjusted upper bound is .95, wider than .88 without the mediator; the authors recommend computing both and taking the tighter upper bound. A 2022 journal follow-up by Dawid, Humphreys, and Musio, [Bounding Causes of Effects With Mediators](https://doi.org/10.1177/00491241211036161), generalizes complete mediation to binary chains and concludes that mediator evidence can help but its gains are often modest and asymmetric, even in idealized known-chain conditions.

**Version/provenance warning:** The frame entry is categorized by arXiv submission date in 2014, but the PDF delivered at the `v1` URL has a manuscript date of February 28, 2022. The later text must not be treated as evidence available in 2014. This audit records both the frame identifier and the document-body date; any historical feature/outcome study must resolve the version chronology before assigning a cutoff.

**Interpretation and limits:** The common theme is not “mediators always improve causal inference.” Complete mediation permits one result; partial mediation can widen the upper bound; the later chain analysis still finds modest gains. All require strong structural and exchangeability/no-confounding assumptions, often binary variables, and a precisely stated cause-of-effect estimand. A sharper interval is not the same as an identified individual counterfactual.

**Falsifiable follow-up:** Across simulated and real application-specific causal graphs, compare bounds using outcome-only, complete-mediator, and partial-mediator information under controlled direct effects and confounding violations. Measure interval width, coverage of known simulation truth, and sensitivity to mediator measurement error; predefine when mediator information worsens the interval and compare against the bound that ignores it.

## Cross-paper synthesis

The efficiency and mediation papers concern different domains, so no effect sizes are combined. Both expose a hidden condition behind an appealing aggregate claim: a policy that saves compute may fail an accuracy/deadline target, and added causal-process information may fail to tighten an individual-attribution interval. The more defensible research direction is to report competing outcomes and assumption-sensitive counterconditions, not a single “better” score.
