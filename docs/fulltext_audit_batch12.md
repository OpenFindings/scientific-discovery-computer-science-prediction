# Full-text audit batch 12 — when adaptive inference works, and when it breaks

Updated 2026-10-11. This audit extends the fixed-frame uniform-random sample with two model-efficiency papers, then follows their claims across independent papers on corruption shift, edge systems, and difficulty-adaptive exits. The comparison is qualitative: architectures, datasets, policies, and metrics differ, so percentages are not pooled.

## 1. Sampled 2018 paper: early exits find real savings, but the oracle is not deployable

**Frame sample:** Kaya, Hong, and Dumitras, [Shallow-Deep Networks: Understanding and Mitigating Network Overthinking](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1810.07052v3), arXiv:1810.07052v3, submitted 2018; ICML 2019 proceedings. Full-text sections on architecture, thresholds, results, and backdoor analysis were checked.

The paper adds six internal classifiers to VGG-16, ResNet-56, WRN-32-4, and MobileNet, tested on CIFAR-10/100 and Tiny ImageNet. It reports that a confidence-triggered early-exit policy can reduce FLOPs by over 50% on the CIFAR tasks, generally while preserving or improving accuracy; Tiny ImageNet savings are usually over 25% without accuracy loss. But the much larger headline ceiling—roughly 73% lower cost on CIFAR-10, 55% on CIFAR-100, and 40% on Tiny ImageNet—uses an *oracle* that knows whether each intermediate prediction is correct. That label-aware oracle is an upper bound, not a deployable policy. Difficult examples can be correct at an intermediate exit yet have confidence around 0.3 rather than around 0.6 for other correct predictions, so a confidence gate can miss savings. Added exit heads also increase model parameters/storage substantially (the MobileNet example grows about 3.4M to 11.2M parameters with the reported feature-reduction setup).

The backdoor result is a single VGG-16/CIFAR-10 attack setup: the paper reports 92% clean accuracy and 12% poisoned-input accuracy at the full model; an internal exit correctly classifies 87% of attack inputs, and the chosen early-exit policy raises their accuracy to 84% while reducing target-class outputs from 98% to 17%. This demonstrates a possible mitigation in that setup, not a general backdoor defense. The experiments are image-classification benchmarks; no distribution-shift evaluation is reported.

## 2. Independent shift test: the ideal exit is much better than the practical exit

**Citation follow-up:** Mehra et al., [Understanding the Robustness of Multi-Exit Models under Common Corruptions](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2212.01562), arXiv:2212.01562v1 (2022 preprint). Full paper, including main results and stated mechanism, checked.

On CIFAR-10/100-C, VGG-16 and ResNet-56 with six exits, the authors compare confidence, patience, and nearest-neighbor routing against a label-aware first-correct-exit oracle. Under corruption, the oracle saves about 40% compute on average and improves accuracy by at least 10% versus using only the last exit. Practical policies still save compute, but improve accuracy by only about 1% over the last exit; the gap between practical routing and oracle accuracy widens by an average of 5 percentage points under shift. They attribute much of the damage to miscalibrated exits: overconfident early exits cause “underthinking” (leaving before the first correct exit), while cross-exit prediction inconsistency contributes to “overthinking.” AugMix-based training reduces calibration error and these behaviors in the tested setups; test-time batch-normalization adaptation raises accuracy but changes the under/overthinking measures more subtly.

This is a concrete countercondition to treating the 2018 oracle savings as an expected deployment benefit. It is still bounded: common image corruptions and two CNN backbones, not arbitrary semantic/domain shifts, production streams, or all exit controllers. The authors' corruption-based mechanism is plausible and experimentally probed, but does not establish that calibration alone solves routing under shift.

## 3. 2025 edge system: system-level gains depend on the accounting boundary

**Frame sample:** Mounesan, Zhang, and Debroy, [Infer-EDGE: Dynamic DNN Inference Optimization in “Just-in-time” Edge-AI Implementations](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.18842v1), arXiv:2501.18842v1. The paper uses A2C to choose model version and split layer based on workload, network, battery, and latency/accuracy targets. Its testbed includes three Jetson TX2 devices, a Dell server, and a USRP B210; inference is evaluated with VGG/ResNet/DenseNet variants. The headline reports up to 77% lower latency and 92% lower device energy without accuracy compromise in selected multi-objective configurations.

The engineering boundary matters: UAV mobility energy is simulated, video-capture energy is excluded, and server energy is excluded from device-energy accounting. Workload scheduling uses 30-second slots, three simulated UAVs, Poisson task arrivals, four cutpoints per model/version, and 5,000 A2C episodes. Latency/energy gains move with objective weights and network conditions; the result is a useful measured-device-plus-simulation study, not a full end-to-end energy audit of a deployed fleet.

**Version dependence / not an independent replication:** The frame-sampled Infer-EDGE arXiv record itself notes substantial text overlap with the authors’ earlier [EdgeRL](https://arxiv.org/html/2410.12221v1) submission. The earlier work is a related version/project, not independent corroboration; Infer-EDGE expands the profiling and workload/activity analysis. Both are retained for provenance and progression, but counted as one research line when assessing evidence independence.

## 4. 2026 adaptive thresholds: “difficulty-aware” is promising, not yet broad evidence

**Citation follow-up:** Patne et al., [DART: Input-Difficulty-AwaRe Adaptive Threshold for Early-Exit DNNs](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2603.12269v1), arXiv:2603.12269v1 (2026). The method combines hand-engineered image-difficulty features, joint threshold optimization, and online coefficient adaptation. Experiments cover AlexNet, ResNet-18, VGG-16 and LeViT on MNIST/CIFAR-10; the reported platform is an A100 GPU, not an edge-device energy test.

The table-level results qualify the broad “competitive accuracy” summary. On CIFAR-10, reported DART accuracy is 82.86% vs 85.29% for static AlexNet (1.60× speedup), 85.35% vs 88.32% for ResNet-18 (2.25×), and 80.20% vs 79.16% for VGG-16 (3.33×). On MNIST AlexNet it reports 99.31% vs 98.97% (3× speedup). On LeViT/CIFAR-10, the speedups are around 2.5–3.6× but some configurations lose roughly 10–17 accuracy points; the authors themselves call for transformer-specific exits. Difficulty coefficients and thresholds are tuned on validation data, and an online class-aware update uses high-confidence model predictions as pseudo-labels when true labels are absent. That creates a validation question about feedback under misclassification; it is not, by itself, evidence that the adaptation fails.

## Cross-paper finding

Across the 2018 sampled SDN paper, the 2022 independent corruption-shift study, the 2025 sampled edge framework, and the 2026 DART follow-up, the durable point is not simply “adaptive inference saves compute.” It is: *the attainable saving depends on whether the controller can identify which exit will be correct, whether the evaluation distribution matches calibration data, which model family is used, and what energy/latency boundary is counted.* Oracle and practical results must be separated; distribution shift and transformer backbones expose accuracy regressions; related preprint versions must not be mistaken for independent replications.

This is evidence of a recurring evaluation gap, not a prevalence estimate and not a novelty claim. The 2026 ICML paper [Rethinking Calibration for Early-Exit Neural Networks](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v306/kubaty26a.html) is an additional high-priority follow-up: its abstract states that calibration alone does not ensure good adaptive computation and proposes predicting exit failure while accounting for the cost of further computation. Its [official repository](https://github.com/gmum/rethinking-calibration-for-eenns) is public and MIT-licensed; the README lists training, calibration, confidence-corrector, and comparison scripts, with ResNet/ViT architectures and CIFAR-100, TinyImageNet, ImageNet, and DomainNet data paths. A spot-check of `eval.py` confirms threshold sweeps measure accuracy and FLOPs, and the code includes an exit-compute-saving AUROC. The PDF itself was not machine-readable in the available browser fetch, and no code was run here; therefore the empirical numerical claims remain abstract-level only and this is not counted as a completed full-text audit. The open reproduction assets are useful, but precise dataset/model/result coverage and train/test protocol still need paper/code comparison.

**Falsifiable next study:** Using shared CNN and ViT/multi-exit checkpoints, compare raw confidence, temperature scaling, failure-probability routing, and difficulty-adaptive routing under the same validation labels and compute/tuning budget. Evaluate clean, common-corruption, and at least one semantic/domain-shift test; report accuracy, risk-coverage/outage, FLOPs, measured tail latency, and device plus server energy separately. Include an oracle only as an upper bound, measure exit-controller selection bias, and preregister the acceptable accuracy and deadline constraints. For edge claims, include camera, communication, server, and device energy or label every omitted component. Replicate across seeds and disclose related/versioned papers.
