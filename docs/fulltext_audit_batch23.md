# Full-text audit batch 23: energy numbers need a declared accounting boundary

Updated 2026-10-11. This is a four-paper slice from the predeclared fixed-frame uniform-random `energy_efficient_computing` sample: one paper per arXiv-submission period. It includes experimental energy estimates, a worst-case complexity result, an assumed-power calculation, and a simulation paper reporting Wh. These evidence types answer different questions and should not be ranked as if their energy numbers had a common measurement basis.

## 1. Data Placement and Replica Selection for Improving Co-location in Distributed Environments (2013)

[Kumar, Deshpande, and Khuller, arXiv:1302.4168v1](https://arxiv.org/abs/1302.4168); [full arXiv PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1302.4168v1), §1, §5, discussion and conclusion.

The paper tries to reduce query span—the number of partitions touched by a query—by colocating frequently co-accessed data and selecting replicas. Its motivating MySQL/TPC-H and custom query-processor experiments do not directly meter energy: they estimate it using an Itanium-server power model built with Mantis, with `dstat` CPU, network, I/O, and memory counters as inputs. In those small experiments, estimated energy rose as query span/parallelism increased. Importantly, the TPC-H query runtimes themselves increased with more machines, which the authors attribute as a possibility to MySQL Cluster's nested-loop join implementation; the custom processor's runtime stayed roughly constant. The paper separately evaluates placement algorithms on synthetic and real workloads, including a trace-driven simulator and an Amazon EC2 setup.

**Boundary and counterweight:** the energy conclusion is a model-derived estimate, not a wall-plug meter reading or a carbon-footprint measurement. Query span is used as a proxy for resource use; it does not directly optimize all the interacting costs. Lower span may increase individual-query latency and can create load imbalance; the method also assumes the captured historical workload is predictive, and its main formulation is read-only oriented. So “more parallelism always wastes energy” is not supported: observed runtime behavior varies by implementation and workload, while the paper's point is specifically that query-span/resource tradeoffs should be evaluated for latency-tolerant workloads.

## 2. On the Limitations of Analysing Worst-Case Dynamic Energy of Processing (2016 arXiv; 2018 journal version)

[Morse, Kerrison, and Eder, arXiv:1603.02580v4](https://arxiv.org/abs/1603.02580); [full arXiv HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/1603.02580), §§2–8.

This is a theory paper about the worst-case dynamic energy caused by operand-dependent switching in processor datapaths, not a benchmark of average energy-saving techniques. It proves exact worst-case switching is NP-hard and rules out useful constant- or polynomial-factor approximation with guaranteed safety under the formal model. Thus a safe upper bound may have to be coarse/over-pessimistic; an average instruction-energy model based on random operands is not automatically a safe worst-case bound. The paper's Xcore case study shows operand-dependent switching can be material on a deeply embedded processor.

**Counterweight and scope:** the result concerns one component of dynamic processor energy, particularly relevant when processor datapath switching is a substantial fraction of system energy. The authors note that in systems with caches, memory hierarchies, displays, peripherals, and multiple supplies, this contribution can be a smaller share of total energy. The theorem is not a claim that average workload energy cannot be measured or optimized; worst-case safety and typical/mean energy are different targets. The paper itself points toward statistical characterization as a separate route, without treating it as a safe worst-case guarantee.

## 3. Dual-pronged Deep Learning Preprocessing on Heterogeneous Platforms with CPU, Accelerator and CSD (2024)

[Wei et al., arXiv:2407.00005v2](https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.00005); [full arXiv PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2407.00005v2), §§VI-A–C and conclusion.

DDLP overlaps preprocessing on CPU and a computable storage device (CSD) with accelerator training. The paper reports speed improvements and energy reductions for ImageNet/CIFAR-10 experiments, with the precise energy result depending on configuration and worker count. It says energy is calculated as processor power multiplied by time: the host CPU is assigned 200 W across 40 threads, one process 5 W, the 16 additional processes 85 W, and the CSD 0.25 W. The paper reports averages over 20 seeds. CSD-only preprocessing can consume less energy in this accounting, but significantly increases learning time; the proposed overlapped methods aim for a speed/energy compromise.

**Boundary and counterweight:** these are calculation results under stated power assumptions, not a whole-system wall-plug measurement. The current CSD is emulated with a Pynq platform, and the experiment explicitly ignores the transfer time between Pynq's external storage and host external storage. The setup therefore does not capture every path cost or prove the same gains on production CSD hardware. Moreover, the stated objective prioritizes speed and energy as reported; a different latency deadline or energy-only objective can choose a different point on the tradeoff curve.

## 4. Performance and Energy Trade-Off Analysis of Hierarchical Federated Learning for Plant Disease Classification (2026)

[Papanikolaou et al., arXiv:2605.08121v1](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.08121); [full arXiv PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2605.08121v1), §§III–V and Tables I–II.

The paper evaluates simulated hierarchical federated-learning configurations over ten clients, 30 communication rounds and five local epochs, using PlantDoc and one NVIDIA RTX 6000 Ada system. Table II reports whole-experiment energy in Wh, execution time, and classification metrics for nine model/aggregator combinations. The best-accuracy configuration (ResNet-50 + FedAvg) is also highest energy in this table (575.12 Wh); MobileNetV3-Large + FedProx is lowest energy (142.73 Wh), while MobileNetV3-Large + FedAvgM is fastest. This is useful evidence that “best” depends on whether the objective is diagnostic performance, energy, or wall-clock time.

**Reproducibility limit:** although the paper identifies the GPU and says energy is a logged/system-level measurement for the complete experiment, its six-page methods do not state the energy acquisition/calculation procedure, meter/counter, system components included, idle-baseline treatment, or repeated-run variance. The Wh values therefore cannot be independently reconstructed from the paper alone. This is a reporting gap, not evidence that the values are wrong. The experiment is explicitly simulated FL on one GPU, with balanced i.i.d. client partitions; it does not directly measure ten geographically distributed farm devices or their network/communication energy. Its F1/Wh ratio and normalized weighted objective are useful within the stated candidate set, but do not replace an explicit deployment constraint or a transparent energy ledger.

## Bounded cross-paper finding

In this four-paper slice, “energy” denotes at least four distinct constructs: modeled operational estimate from system counters; safe worst-case dynamic-energy bound; assumed component power multiplied by runtime; and a reported total Wh value without enough acquisition detail to reproduce it. The methods cannot be collapsed into a single efficiency ranking. The sample also shows meaningful counterevidence to an overly broad measurement-gap story: the 2013 paper discloses its model and input counters, the 2024 paper gives its assumed wattage and exposes a storage-transfer omission, and the 2016 paper precisely defines a theoretically different worst-case target. The weakest reproducibility is the 2026 paper's unspecified route from one-GPU experiment to Table II Wh values.

**Falsifiable follow-up:** audit a larger, stratified sample of energy-related systems papers and classify each claim by estimand (mean/task energy, whole-system energy, marginal energy, or safe worst case), measurement source (meter, hardware counter, calibrated model, assumed power, or unspecified), included components and time window, workload and quality/deadline achieved, calibration/uncertainty, and repeat count. Then have independent groups reproduce/rerank a subset using the published accounting contract. The hypothesis that explicit accounting improves reproducibility and prevents invalid rankings is weakened if these fields do not predict replication success or if rankings remain stable under reasonable boundary/meter changes. Include examples where measurement is well disclosed and where energy is outside the paper's target, rather than treating every missing Wh figure as a defect.

This is a four-item hypothesis-generating sample, not a prevalence estimate, historical trend, pooled energy-saving effect, or novelty claim.
