# Full-text audit batch 31: embodied-robot results depend on what “success” counts

Updated 2026-10-12. This batch audits four unread random-frame records from the later periods of `robotics_embodied_ai`: two from 2020–2024 and two from the 2025–2026-through-2026-10-11 stratum. Four of this cell's ten unread items are now covered; six remain. The papers address sparse-reward learning, social navigation, open-vocabulary legged navigation, and humanoid recovery from acrobatics. Their outcome measures are intentionally kept separate.

## 2020–2024

### 1. HiER: Highlight Experience Replay for Boosting Off-Policy Reinforcement Learning Agents (2023; sampled v3)

[Exact sampled HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2312.09394v3), method, evaluation protocol, aggregate results, and conclusion.

HiER places successful/high-return episodes in a second replay buffer and samples from both it and the ordinary buffer. Across eight tasks in three robotics benchmarks and two simulators, the paper reports ten independent random-seed runs per algorithm/task. The matched SAC+HER comparison raises aggregate IQM success rate from 0.56 to 0.83; the authors also report non-overlapping confidence intervals for that comparison. HiER is additionally paired with an easy-to-hard initial-state curriculum on three Panda-Gym tasks, and variants are tested with SAC, TD3, and DDPG.

**Counterweight and boundary:** all eight evaluations are simulated; the authors explicitly leave sim-to-sim/sim-to-real transfer to future work. The best per-run evaluation score is used for success-rate aggregate metrics, which is closer to selecting each run's best checkpoint than measuring a final deployed policy. The improvement is about benchmark task completion, not physical-robot safety or transfer.

## 2025–2026-through-2026-10-11

### 2. GSON: A Group-based Social Navigation Framework with Large Multimodal Model (2024; sampled v3)

[Exact sampled HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2409.18084v3), social-group benchmark, real-robot experiment, dense-crowd limitations, and conclusion.

GSON uses a vision-language model to infer pedestrian social groups, then plans around those groups. On 100 annotated image/video scenarios (ten model calls per scene), GPT-4o reaches 77.8% exact grouping and 86.6% when acceptable omissions are included. In physical mobile-robot tests, scenarios include queues, conversation, and photography; each was run three times. GSON has the lowest reported group/individual disturbance among its selected baselines while remaining broadly comparable on conventional motion metrics. The paper also reports a roughly 100-m long-range demonstration.

**Counterweight and boundary:** LMM calls add latency—about 1.5–2 seconds for the chosen Qwen2.5-VL-32B path—and in dense crowds camera occlusions and the binary “group/not-group” representation cause incomplete perception or overly conservative avoidance. Several other LMM navigation baselines were excluded because they were not open source or did not work reliably in the authors' pilot reproductions. The real-robot comparisons use a small number of repeated scenarios; they are valuable physical evidence, not a large human-subject safety study.

### 3. LOVON: Legged Open-Vocabulary Object Navigator (2025; sampled v1)

[Exact sampled HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2507.06747v1), blur filtering, simulation comparison, real-robot experiments, and ablations.

LOVON combines an LLM task planner, open-vocabulary detection, motion-state handling, and a blur filter for legged-robot cameras. The authors generate a one-million-example training set with LLM-expanded synonyms/instructions, then evaluate four Gym-Unreal scenes over 100 trials each: reported success rates are 0.99–1.00, at 1.5 training hours versus 360 hours for the listed TrackVLA comparator. On physical robot approach tests, Laplacian filtering plus confidence smoothing raises the “qualified frame” rate about 25%; in a Go2 search ablation, 6-m backpack search falls from 178 seconds without the filter/extra state to 32.57 seconds with both.

**Counterweight and boundary:** the strongest numerical multi-scene results are simulation results. Real-world evidence is a collection of demonstrations and targeted Go2 ablations, not a repeated multi-robot success-rate study, even though the paper describes compatibility across Go2, B2, and H1-2. The data are largely generated by an LLM and the frame-rejection threshold is empirically calibrated; too high a threshold can discard valid frames. Results depend on model size, data volume, loss weight, and a special separator token. Open-world robustness claims therefore need a controlled test set with unseen objects, lighting, floors, and perturbations.

### 4. Continue, Abort, or Fall: Viability-Aware Policy Selection (VAPS) for Safe Humanoid Acrobatics (2026; sampled v1)

[Exact sampled HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2610.01397v1), specialist-policy design, predictor evaluation, simulation tradeoffs, and hardware trials.

VAPS combines a task-tracking policy, a controlled-abort policy, and a protective-fall policy; learned viability predictors decide whether the robot should continue, abort, or protect itself. In randomized-disturbance simulation on Unitree G1, full VAPS reduces head contact from 27.7% for nominal-only to 1.6%, and hand contact from 36.6% to 8.8%; task success drops from 62.1% to 59.6% at the shared alarm threshold. On LimX Oli, the paper reports 18 full-hierarchy hardware trials: the selected specialist achieved its intended objective in 15 (83.3%). Separate manually triggered backup-policy tests succeed in 17/19 aborts and protect head/hands in 12/16 protective falls. The learned predictor also labels 13 real side-flip trials correctly, but that is a small validation set.

**Counterweight and boundary:** this is stronger hardware evidence than a simulator-only result, but trial counts remain small and the task is one acrobatic maneuver on named humanoid platforms. The explicit safety/task tradeoff is important: reducing contact can lower motion success; the best operating points form a Pareto frontier rather than one universally best score. The authors' own hardware numbers show failures in three of 18 end-to-end VAPS trials, so “safe” here means substantial measured reduction in selected impact proxies, not a guarantee of no damage.

## Cross-paper synthesis: report a vector of outcomes, not one robotics score

These four papers provide both positive evidence and a useful counterexample to broad claims. HiER has a statistically reported improvement across eight tasks but no physical transfer test. GSON and LOVON show that physical demonstration is possible, yet their real-world trial designs and endpoints differ (social disturbance versus object tracking/search). VAPS measures impact/contact explicitly and exposes a success-versus-protection tradeoff. It would be invalid to pool any of their headline numbers into a single “robot autonomy” trend.

### Candidate research finding: common embodied evaluations should jointly score task progress and harm

There is a testable gap between task-completion benchmarks and task-specific harm measures. Existing examples separately show why both matter: social navigation can reach a destination while disturbing a group; object navigation can track successfully while camera blur causes loss/re-search; acrobatic safety can reduce head/hand contacts while sacrificing a small amount of maneuver success. This does **not** establish that no shared evaluation exists elsewhere, nor that these systems fail in deployment.

**Falsifiable test:** build a preregistered physical-robot evaluation with matched scenarios and robots, and measure a vector of task success, completion time, human/social disturbance or contact forces, near misses, recovery/abort rate, perception loss, intervention rate, and energy/compute. Use repeated trials across lighting, occlusion, terrain, crowd density, and controlled disturbances; independently adjudicate harm events and publish all failed runs. The gap hypothesis weakens if existing shared protocols already cover these outcomes and methods retain their rankings across tasks, settings, and physical replications.
