Updated 2026-10-12. This batch covers the three remaining lexical-priority records in the frozen `algorithms_complexity × 2010–2014` cell. ## Sampled papers

### 1. Submodular Inference of Diffusion Networks from Multiple Trees (arXiv:1205.1671v1; first submitted 2012)

[arXiv record](https://arxiv.org/abs/1205.1671); [official ICML 2012 PDF](https://icml.cc/2012/papers/281.pdf); locally retrieved PDF: `data/fulltexts/arxiv/1205.1671v1.pdf` (8 pages); extracted text: `data/fulltexts/text/1205.1671v1.txt`.

The paper formulates likelihood-based inference from observed cascade times as a monotone submodular maximization problem. Unlike NetInf, it sums over all propagation trees consistent with a cascade, and uses greedy optimization with lazy evaluation. In synthetic networks (including 1,024-node Kronecker graphs) the method often has higher recall than NetInf when only a small number of cascades are observed; performance differences shrink as cascades accumulate, and NetRate is better on one hierarchical synthetic topology. On the MemeTracker-derived real-data experiment, “ground truth” edges are constructed from hyperlinks among the 1,000 most active sites; this is a measurable proxy for information flow, not direct observation of who influenced whom. The model assumes a fixed underlying network, even though the motivation emphasizes rapidly changing networks. A scale experiment reports 10.12 and 12.14 ms per added edge on 100,000- and 200,000-node synthetic networks with 10,000 cascades; that demonstrates one model-specific throughput result, not recovery of an evolving real-world graph.

### 2. Faster Private Release of Marginals on Small Databases (arXiv:1304.3754v2; first submitted 2013; ITCS 2014)

[arXiv record and abstract](https://arxiv.org/abs/1304.3754); [ITCS 2014 publication record](https://privacytools.seas.harvard.edu/publications/faster-private-release-marginals-small-databases); locally retrieved PDF: `data/fulltexts/arxiv/1304.3754v2.pdf` (27 pages); extracted text: `data/fulltexts/text/1304.3754v2.txt`.

Theorem 1.1 gives an `(ε,δ)`-differentially private online algorithm for `k`-way marginals over records in `{0,1}^d`. It answers adaptively chosen queries with additive error at most `0.01` on every query with probability at least `0.99`, provided the database size scales as `n ≥ C d^0.51 log(|Q|) log(1/δ)/ε`; per-query runtime is polynomial in `n` and subexponential in `d`, with the stated exponent depending on `k`. An offline corollary releases all `k`-way marginals under its own database-size condition. The result is a worst-case theoretical guarantee, not a measured deployment: runtime can still be exponential-like in dimension, and privacy, accuracy, query count, and database size trade against each other. The paper explicitly notes that private multiplicative weights had shown practical performance on some databases, while independent-noise methods could fail on those databases; its improvement targets the runtime/database-size regime, not universal practical superiority. Its polynomial lower bound constrains the paper's low-weight polynomial representation approach, not every possible differentially private mechanism.

### 3. Sparse similarity-preserving hashing (arXiv:1312.5479v3; first submitted 2013, checked version dated 2014)

[arXiv HTML full text](https://arxiv.org/html/1312.5479v3); locally retrieved PDF: `data/fulltexts/arxiv/1312.5479v3.pdf` (13 pages); extracted text: `data/fulltexts/text/1312.5479v3.txt`.

SparseHash uses an `ℓ1`-regularized ISTA-style neural encoder to produce high-dimensional sparse binary codes. The paper evaluates CIFAR-10 and NUS-WIDE using precision/recall, F1, mAP, and retrieval time, plus image-tag cross-modal retrieval. It reports higher recall at similar precision than dense codes with comparable degrees of freedom; on CIFAR-10, 128-bit sparse codes are about 6% nonzero on average. But the paper's own system-level test shows the operating boundary: with 48-bit codes, lookup-table retrieval at Hamming radii 0 and 1 is faster, while at radius 2 brute-force search is faster. Its similarity labels are dataset-defined (e.g., shared CIFAR class or NUS concept), so good retrieval on those labels is not a general guarantee of semantic relevance. The CIFAR protocol uses 200 training images per class and the remainder as the retrieval database; NUS uses 100,000 training pairs. This evidence supports the method on those benchmarks, not an across-workload claim of lower end-to-end cost.

## Bounded, falsifiable research directions

These are three distinct leads, not one universal gap:

1. **Diffusion inference under network drift.** Compare fixed-network inference with a rolling-window or change-point-aware method on synthetic cascades where edge structure changes at controlled rates, then on time-stamped real cascade snapshots where possible. Report edge precision/recall against time-local ground truth, lag after a topology change, calibration of transmission rates, and compute per update. The lead weakens if fixed-network inference remains as accurate and timely across drift rates; it strengthens if an explicit temporal model improves held-out time-local recovery without unacceptable lag or compute. The MemeTracker hyperlink construction is not treated as causal ground truth.
2. **Privacy/runtime regimes under adaptive workloads.** For a fixed family of marginal queries, benchmark the theorem's parameterized runtime and accuracy against private multiplicative weights and noise baselines over database size, `d`, `k`, `ε`, `δ`, and adaptive-query count. Keep privacy accounting and utility separate; verify the claimed privacy mechanism, not just empirical noise. The lead weakens if the asymptotic advantage does not appear in relevant finite regimes or the baseline dominates; it strengthens if the small-database regime yields repeatable wall-clock gains at matched privacy and error. No implementation or privacy test was run here.
3. **Index-aware sparse-hash evaluation.** Compare SparseHash and dense baselines under the same training labels, code storage budget, index implementation, and query set, sweeping code length and Hamming radius. Report recall/precision/mAP together with p50/p95 latency, candidates visited, index memory, and build/update cost. The lead weakens if sparsity's recall gain cannot translate into a better quality-latency-memory frontier after the index switches to brute force; it strengthens if it does across held-out datasets and similarity definitions. No model was rerun.
