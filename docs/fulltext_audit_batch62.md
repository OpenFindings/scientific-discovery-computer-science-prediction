## 1. MAS-ProVe: Understanding the Process Verification of Multi-Agent Systems (`2602.03053v2`)

- **Research question and method:** Can intermediate-step verifiers steer MAS reliably? The paper compares LLM-as-a-Judge, reward models and process reward models; agent-call vs iteration-level selection; four context treatments; and six MAS frameworks. At each step it samples three continuations and greedily keeps the verifier's highest-ranked candidate. Main tasks include AIME24/25, GAIA information extraction, HumanEval and GPQA. AIME24/25 each have 30 questions; GAIA's evaluated information-extraction subset has 103. The main table reports means and standard deviations over three experimental runs.
- **Evidence supporting usefulness:** Some paired settings improve substantially over the no-verifier baseline: e.g., Debate with GPT-5-mini judge is 81.11% vs 74.13% on AIME24 and 62.97% vs 61.87% on AIME25; other MAS/task/granularity combinations also gain. The paper's selected best judge/context configurations improve stability on some hard-but-not-impossible questions. LLM judges beat RM/PRM in 24/36 configurations in the reported comparison.
- **Counterevidence / boundary:** There is no monotonic general gain. In Table 1, verifier/model/granularity changes often reduce performance, and the preferred granularity changes by architecture and benchmark. The paper reports that its best verifier configurations rarely recover questions that fail across baseline trials. The N=3 greedy candidate search, three-run comparison and small AIME sets do not establish that arbitrary process-verification policies work. For GAIA, the tool/prompt/operator setup was substantially adapted; HumanEval uses a different generator and therefore is not a uniform model-controlled replication.
- **Finding from this source:** Treat process verification as a conditional search heuristic, not a generally reliable quality gate. Expected value should be measured against a no-verifier baseline with fixed model, context, candidate budget, architecture and multiple seeds; report task-level reversals and verifier cost.
- **Sections checked:** abstract/introduction; framework and greedy selection (Sec. 3); Tables 1–5 and results (Secs. 4.1–4.4); solvability analysis; conclusion; benchmark counts and baseline adaptations (Appendix A.1–A.2); context and extra results (Appendix B).
- **Version/status:** arXiv v2, revised 2026-08-31; listed as ICML 2026 Regular on arXiv. [Primary record](https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.03053).

## 2. Evolving Idea Graphs with Learnable Edits-and-Commits for Multi-Agent Scientific Ideation (`2605.04922v2`)

- **Research question and method:** Does persistent, typed graph state plus a learned edit/commit controller improve multi-agent scientific ideation over text-centric methods? EIG maintains claim/method/evaluation nodes and typed support/conflict/dependency edges; a two-head controller chooses edits and whether to stop. The evaluation uses AI Idea Bench 2025 and LiveIdeaBench, a fixed 512-group held-out subset (256 per benchmark), Qwen3-8B in the main run, and matched input/output schemas. A 400-group heuristic-teacher corpus trains the controller.
- **Evidence supporting usefulness:** EIG reports a 7.41 mean benchmark score vs 6.95 for AI-Researcher on the paper's normalized scale, using 13.75k traced tokens/sample; the authors report graph/controller ablation gains. A blinded expert study on 24 groups uses three reviewers/group from a 12-person pool. EIG's mean overall rating is 4.01 vs 3.50 for VirSci, and average rank 1.64 vs 2.92; paired intervals exclude zero for those summary outcomes. Cross-backbone checks include DeepSeek-V3.2 and GPT-4.1-mini.
- **Counterevidence / boundary:** Human evaluation covers 24 groups, not all 512; novelty, feasibility and context-adherence intervals include zero, and the paper treats those dimensions as directional rather than established wins. The controller learns from protocol-matched heuristic labels, not expert actions or experimentally validated outcomes. Most importantly, this tests proposal quality at the ideation stage, not whether proposals survive implementation or improve scientific results. Its own limitation section expressly separates those downstream questions.
- **Finding from this source:** Structured proposal state and adaptive stopping/editing are promising for benchmark-defined ideation, but the scores do not show end-to-end discovery or execution success. A stronger test should preserve the graph/controller comparison while implementing proposals, then compare predicted quality with preregistered outcomes.
- **Sections checked:** setup and split hygiene (Sec. 4.1 and Appendix F); main tables and score definitions (Sec. 4.2); ablations and cross-backbone checks (Secs. 4.3–4.4 and Appendix I); human evaluation and uncertainty protocol (Appendix J); explicit limitations (Appendix L).
- **Version/status:** arXiv v2, revised 2026-09-26. [Primary record](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.04922).

## 3. LatticeMind: A Conflict-Aware Memory Primitive for Multi-Agent Systems (`2608.08236v1`)

- **Research question and method:** Does writing disagreement into structured persistent memory outperform answer-level aggregation? LatticeMind tracks claim status/provenance, runs symbolic conflict checks, and asks an LLM to reconcile unresolved semantic conflicts. On label-blind ConflictBank, all methods see the same three evidence sources under neutral Source A/B/C labels and use Qwen3-Max.
- **Evidence supporting usefulness:** On the 75-example ConflictBank evaluation, the paper reports 0.97 accuracy (73/75) vs 0.63 for single-agent inference and 0.61 for majority vote, with paired McNemar p<1e-6. A 50-example ablation reports 0.96 full accuracy, 0.84 without the checker, and 0.82 without the reconciler. In the separate software-state benchmark, explicit memory improves provenance and temporal-query scores over the included summary baselines under a capped rendered-memory budget.
- **Counterevidence / boundary:** This is strongest evidence for a narrow credibility-conflict setting, not all multi-agent memory. Four secondary planning benchmarks are tiny (n=10–15 each): LatticeMind loses to No-Merge on TravelPlanner (0.80 vs 0.87), ties single-agent on REALM JSSP (0.20), and does not replace deliberation on iterative-search tasks. The paper's software-state benchmark is constructed for the study, and its real repository bridge uses two Codex trajectories; that bridge is not broad production validation. The authors state deployments in high-stakes settings need additional validation. Another necessary boundary is irreducible conflict: a memory system should preserve competing claims/ask for context instead of always choosing a winner. TANGLE (arXiv:2608.13921) is a relevant scope counterpoint at abstract level; it is not a full-text audit in this batch.
- **Finding from this source:** Explicit conflict state can outperform answer aggregation when evidence contains resolvable credibility conflicts and the task rewards maintaining a current state. It does not establish that forced reconciliation is safe when evidence is ambiguous, time-/scope-dependent, or genuinely underdetermined.
- **Sections checked:** abstract/introduction; memory/update mechanism (Sec. 3); benchmark protocol and sample sizes (Secs. 4.1–4.3); secondary task boundary (Sec. 4.4); software-state and two-trajectory replay study; limitations; reproducibility/model disclosures (Appendix A).
- **Version/status:** arXiv v1, submitted 2026-08-08. [Primary record](https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.08236).

## Cross-paper synthesis and next falsifiable test

Across these three 2026 studies, persistent/structured state appears most useful when the task exposes a concrete intermediate object to edit (a proposal graph or conflicting claim store). It is not evidence for a blanket “more agents/memory/verifying is better” rule: MAS-ProVe shows architecture- and task-specific reversals; EIG's positive ideation score is not downstream execution; and LatticeMind's large conflict-resolution gain coexists with small-sample planning regressions and a separate class of irreducible conflicts. These studies have different tasks, metrics and baselines, so their headline scores must not be pooled.

**Testable opportunity:** run a matched factorial study crossing (a) unstructured transcript, typed persistent graph/memory, and answer-only aggregation; (b) no verifier vs process verifier; and (c) resolvable vs genuinely underdetermined conflicts. Hold backbone, evidence, tool access, token/API budget and seed schedule fixed. Measure answer correctness, unsupported supersession, conflict-retention/calibration, human-rated proposal quality, actual implementation success, token cost and failures by task family. The strong universal-memory claim weakens if structured state loses its gain under blinded held-out conflicts or creates more wrong forced updates; it strengthens if it improves correctness and provenance without collapsing unresolved evidence, and predicts downstream implementation outcomes. This batch did not run that experiment and makes no prevalence or novelty claim.
